IUL Lawsuits Are in the News — Here's How to Tell a Well-Designed Policy From a Bad One

If you've searched for IUL lately, you've probably seen headlines about lawsuits over how some policies were sold. The common thread: some people were sold policies using "illustrations" — fancy projections showing how much their cash value would grow — that were about as realistic as a weather forecast for next October. Some of those illustrations leaned on optimistic assumptions, and some clients didn't understand that the projections weren't guaranteed. Insurance regulators have since tightened the rules on how high an illustrated rate can be. Years later, some policyholders are opening statements and finding far less cash value than they expected.

Here's the thing though — none of that means IUL itself is broken, any more than a bad mechanic means all cars are broken. The actual product, done right, still offers something genuinely useful: tax-deferred cash value growth, with a floor that keeps index credits from going below zero in a down market. (Policy fees still apply, so cash value can still go down in a zero-credit year.) The difference between a policy that ends up in a lawsuit and one that does its job over the long run often comes down to two things: whether the illustration was built on realistic, conservative assumptions instead of a best-case fantasy, and whether the person who sold it to you actually explained the caps, fees, and trade-offs instead of just the highlight reel. That's the gap we try to close: conservative illustrations with guaranteed values shown, and plain-English conversations about costs and trade-offs.
