Without a Plan

What Your Family Can
Face Without One

When assets are only in your name with no plan in place, state law and a court often decide what happens next. That can be slower, more public and more expensive than most families expect.

⚖️

Probate court

A house titled only in your name usually has to go through probate before your family can sell it or move it into their names. Depending on the state and the estate, that can take months, sometimes longer, with court and attorney costs along the way.

📄

A public record

Probate filings are generally public. What you owned and who received it can become part of the court record for anyone to look up.

🧒

Money left to minors

Insurance companies generally won't pay a death benefit directly to a minor child. Without a trust or custodian in place, a court may need to appoint someone to manage it, and in many states the child can receive the whole amount outright at 18 or 21.

🏥

If you can't manage your own affairs

If illness or injury leaves you unable to handle your finances, your family may need to ask a court for a guardianship or conservatorship to act for you.

🗺️

Property in more than one state

Own a cabin or land in another state? Your family may face a separate probate case there too.

👨‍👩‍👧

Blended families

Second marriages and stepchildren make "it'll sort itself out" risky. Without clear instructions, the people you meant to provide for may not be the ones who receive it.

What a Living Trust Can Do

Your Instructions.
Your Timeline. Your Family.

A revocable living trust is a legal document you create while you're alive. You move your assets into it, you stay in control as trustee, and you can change it any time. When you pass away or can't manage things, the person you picked steps in and follows your written instructions.

🏡

Pass your home without probate

Assets properly titled in the trust can usually go to your beneficiaries without a probate case. Your successor trustee handles it directly.

🔒

Keep it private

A trust generally isn't filed with a court, so the details of your estate can stay within your family.

🎓

Control when and how money is used

Instead of a lump sum at 18, you can say how money is used: tuition, a first home, a set amount at 25 and the rest at 30. The trustee follows your rules.

🤝

Plan for incapacity

If you become unable to manage your finances, your successor trustee can step in for assets in the trust, often without a court guardianship.

💛

Provide for someone with special needs

A properly drafted special needs trust can help provide for a loved one without disqualifying them from needs-based government benefits. This one needs an attorney.

✏️

Change it as life changes

A revocable trust can be updated or revoked any time you're competent: a new grandchild, a divorce, a move to another state.

Life Insurance + Trust

Who Is the Beneficiary
on Your Policy?

Life insurance pays directly to whoever is named as the beneficiary. It doesn't follow your will. So if your beneficiary form is out of date, or names a child who is still a minor, the money may not go where you meant it to, or the way you meant it to.

Naming your trust as the beneficiary (or contingent beneficiary) sends the death benefit to your trustee, who then follows your trust's instructions: paying off the house, covering school, or holding money until your children are older.

For larger estates, an attorney may recommend an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) to own the policy, so the death benefit isn't counted in your taxable estate. The federal estate tax exemption for 2026 is $15 million per person, so most families won't need this for estate tax reasons alone.

It isn't always the right move. A trust as beneficiary can make sense with minor children, blended families or a loved one with special needs. For a married couple with grown children, naming your spouse first may be simpler. The trust has to exist and be named exactly right on the form. We'll walk through it with you, and your attorney should confirm it fits your plan.

A Tale of Two Families

Same House. Same Policy.
Very Different Year.

A hypothetical example: a parent passes away, leaving a paid-off home and a $500,000 life insurance policy for two children, ages 9 and 13.

No trust

  • The house is in the parent's name only, so it goes through probate before it can be sold or retitled.
  • The policy names the children. Because they're minors, a court may need to appoint someone to manage the money.
  • The process and the family's finances become part of the court record.
  • In many states, each child can receive their remaining share outright at 18.

Funded living trust

  • The house was retitled into the trust, so the successor trustee can handle it without a probate case.
  • The trust is the policy's beneficiary, so the trustee receives the death benefit directly.
  • Everything stays private, handled by the person the parent chose.
  • The money is used for the home, school and living costs, with the rest paid out at the ages the parent picked.

Hypothetical example for illustration only, not a real client. Results depend on your state's laws, how the trust is written and whether assets are properly moved into it.

The Honest Truth

A trust is a powerful tool. It isn't magic. You deserve to know both sides before you buy one.

What a revocable living trust does not do:

  • It doesn't shield your assets from lawsuits or creditors. Because you keep control of a revocable trust, creditors can generally still reach what's in it. True asset protection takes different (usually irrevocable) planning with an attorney.
  • It doesn't cut taxes for most families. A revocable trust doesn't lower income tax, and most estates are well under the federal estate tax exemption.
  • It doesn't, by itself, protect your home from nursing home costs or qualify you for Medicaid. Those rules are complex and state-specific.
  • It only works if you fund it. Your house deed and accounts have to be retitled into the trust. Anything left outside it may still go through probate.
  • It isn't right for everyone. Beneficiary designations, joint ownership and, in many states, transfer-on-death deeds can also avoid probate. A simple will may be enough for some people.
